Overview
This file preserves the useful North Carolina landlord-tenant rules from a third-party guide while removing marketing language, personal anecdotes, and unsupported claims. Where a statute controls, the statute should be treated as more authoritative than this summary.

Security deposits
North Carolina limits residential security deposits based on tenancy length:
- two weeks' rent for week-to-week
- one and one-half months' rent for month-to-month
- two months' rent for terms longer than month-to-month

These limits come from N.C. Gen. Stat. 42-51(b).

Landlords must generally account for and return the remaining balance of the deposit within the statutory timeline set by N.C. Gen. Stat. 42-52.

Eviction and notice
For nonpayment under N.C. Gen. Stat. 42-3, the landlord must demand rent and wait 10 days before filing the eviction case.

North Carolina does not allow self-help eviction.
Landlords generally cannot lawfully remove tenants by:
- changing locks
- shutting off utilities
- removing doors
- using other pressure tactics instead of court

The court process and writ of possession executed by the sheriff are the lawful path to physical removal.

Repairs and habitability
North Carolina landlords must provide fit and habitable premises under N.C. Gen. Stat. 42-42.
The statute requires landlords to:
- comply with applicable housing and building codes to the extent required
- make repairs necessary to keep the premises fit and habitable
- keep common areas safe
- maintain and promptly repair electrical, plumbing, sanitary, heating, ventilating, air conditioning, and other supplied facilities after written notice from the tenant, except in emergencies
- address imminently dangerous conditions within a reasonable time based on severity

Examples of imminently dangerous conditions listed by statute include:
- unsafe wiring
- unsafe floors or steps
- unsafe ceilings or roofs
- lack of potable water
- lack of operable locks on exterior doors
- broken windows or missing ground-level window locks
- lack of heat in required winter conditions
- lack of an operable toilet
- lack of an operable bathtub or shower

Repair remedies
This guide correctly warns that North Carolina is not generally described as a standard repair-and-deduct state.
Tenants should be cautious about withholding rent and should instead document conditions, give notice, and seek legal advice about possible defenses, rent abatement, or claims.

Periodic tenancy notice
For periodic tenancies, N.C. Gen. Stat. 42-14 provides minimum notice periods of:
- 7 days for month-to-month
- 2 days for week-to-week
- 1 month or more for year-to-year
- 60 days for rental of a manufactured home space

These are minimum statutory rules. A lease may require more notice.

Landlord entry
This third-party guide refers to a "reasonable notice" standard for landlord entry, but the exact rule can depend on the lease and circumstances.
This source should not be treated as creating a statewide statutory 24-hour notice rule.

Late fees
North Carolina late fees are governed by statute and lease terms, not just a general reasonableness rule.
For reliable late-fee analysis, the retriever should prefer sources focused specifically on N.C. Gen. Stat. 42-46.

Practical tenant points
Tenants should:
- keep written records of repair requests
- save payment records
- document move-in and move-out conditions
- act quickly if they receive nonpayment or eviction papers

Practical landlord points
Landlords should:
- use written leases
- follow statutory notice and filing timelines
- avoid self-help eviction
- keep clear records for deposits, repairs, and communications

Key takeaways
- North Carolina law is deadline-driven.
- Deposits, eviction timing, and periodic-tenancy notices are governed by statute.
- Self-help eviction is generally unlawful.
- Habitability problems can support defenses or claims, but tenants should document carefully and avoid unsupported self-help remedies.

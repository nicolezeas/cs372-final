Title: NC Lockout or Tenant Rights Resource
URL: https://www.northcarolinalegalservices.org/article/your-landlord-cannot-evict-you-without-a-court-order-heres-what-to-do-when-they-try
Source type: nc_legal
Issue category: lockout

Overview
This resource explains that landlords in North Carolina generally cannot remove residential tenants without using the court process. A sheriff executing a writ of possession is the lawful method of physical removal after court.

What counts as an illegal lockout or self-help eviction
Examples include:
- changing locks or adding a padlock
- blocking entry
- shutting off water, power, or gas to force the tenant out
- removing doors or windows
- making the unit uninhabitable to drive the tenant out
- moving a tenant's belongings to force the tenant out

These tactics are generally described as self-help eviction and are not the lawful substitute for a court-ordered eviction.

Legal rule
North Carolina landlords generally must use the summary ejectment process and then obtain a writ of possession that is executed by the sheriff.
If there is no sheriff carrying out a writ of possession, the removal is generally not lawful.

Immediate steps for a tenant
1. Document the situation right away.
Take photos and video of:
- changed locks
- padlocks
- blocked entrances
- posted notices
- removed doors or windows
- utilities not working

2. Save communications.
Keep texts, emails, notices, and voicemails from the landlord or property manager.

3. Gather proof of tenancy.
Useful records may include:
- the lease
- rent receipts
- payment screenshots
- mail addressed to the tenant at the property
- ID showing the address
- photos of belongings inside the unit

4. Create a timeline.
Write down:
- when the tenant discovered the lockout or shutoff
- what changed
- who was involved
- whether anyone witnessed it

5. Send one short written message to the landlord.
The guide recommends a short factual message stating what happened, the address, and that North Carolina law requires court process.

6. Consider contacting law enforcement for an incident report.
Even if officers describe the matter as civil, the report number or officer information may still help document what happened.

What the legal eviction process looks like
The lawful process usually requires:
1. The landlord files a summary ejectment case.
2. The tenant is served with court papers.
3. A hearing is held.
4. If the landlord wins, the tenant has an appeal period.
5. After the required steps, the landlord may seek a writ of possession.
6. The sheriff executes the writ.

Filing paperwork alone does not mean the tenant has already been lawfully removed.

Appeals
The guide states that after a magistrate's decision, a tenant generally has 10 days to appeal.
During that period, the landlord still cannot personally lock the tenant out.
If the tenant wants to remain in the home during the appeal, bond procedures may apply through the clerk's office.

Retaliation
If the lockout or eviction threat came soon after repair requests, code complaints, or similar protected activity, the tenant may have a retaliatory eviction defense.
Useful evidence includes:
- repair requests
- inspection notices
- photos of bad conditions
- communications showing the timing

Loss log
The guide suggests tracking all losses caused by the illegal action, such as:
- hotel or temporary housing costs
- spoiled food
- transportation costs
- storage costs
- missed work
- medication replacement

Supporting documents may include receipts, photos, and screenshots.

Key takeaways
- A landlord generally cannot lock out a tenant without court process and a sheriff's writ.
- Changed locks, utility shutoffs, and blocked entry are strong warning signs of illegal self-help eviction.
- Early documentation matters.
- Tenants should save proof of tenancy, communications, and all costs caused by the lockout.

What is Small Claims Court?

Small Claims Court is part of the North Carolina court system where people settle disputes regarding property or money worth $10,000 or less. Every county in North Carolina has a Small Claims Court, which is sometimes called Magistrate's Court. The judge, called a magistrate, may or may not be a lawyer. There is no jury. The trial is quick and informal, usually lasting no more than 15 or 30 minutes. You do not have to have a lawyer to represent you in Small Claims Court, but you may have one.

Do You Need a Lawyer?

Before deciding to handle a case without a lawyer, think carefully about whether legal help is needed. If you are facing eviction by your landlord or being sued by a finance company, you may need a lawyer. If you are a low-income person, you may be eligible to get free legal help from Legal Aid of North Carolina. You may call the Legal Aid Helpline at 866-219-5262 or apply for services online at https://www.legalaidnc.org/apply/.

The Eviction Process

The landlord cannot lock out a tenant without going through the court process. A landlord must file court papers, have a hearing in small claims court, and get an eviction order. After the landlord files the court papers, the sheriff mails the papers to the tenant, delivers them to the tenant, or posts the court papers on the front door. The small claims court hearing is usually heard during the same week that the court papers are filed. This means that tenants may not get the court papers in time to get to the hearing, or they may not be prepared to defend against the eviction.

The Eviction Complaint

In North Carolina, the legal term for eviction is "summary ejectment." The case starts when the landlord files a document called a Complaint in Summary Ejectment, which is also called an eviction complaint. In an eviction case, the landlord is the plaintiff and the tenant is the defendant.

In the eviction complaint, the landlord asks the court to order the tenant to move. Often, the landlord also asks the court to make the tenant pay rent, late fees, court costs, money for damage to the property, utility costs, and other costs.

The complaint is served with a summons. A summons is a notice which states the date, time, and place of the hearing.

Defenses and Counterclaims

To keep from being evicted, a tenant needs a legal defense. A tenant will likely need to testify about facts that support the defenses. If there are documents that support the defenses, bring copies to show the magistrate. Printed pictures, texts, or emails can be used in court. A tenant may also give the magistrate a written answer with defenses. Writing defenses may help present the case in an organized and persuasive way.

Possible defenses include:

- Retaliatory eviction: the tenant made a good faith complaint or asked for repairs within the past year and believes the eviction was filed in response.
- Discrimination: the landlord's reason for evicting the tenant is illegal discrimination.
- Problems with the home: the landlord failed to repair problems with the home.
- Waiver: the landlord accepted rent after knowing about lease violations.
- Domestic violence: the landlord filed the eviction because the tenant is a victim of domestic violence.
- Disability: the tenant or a member of the household has a disability.
- Notice: the landlord failed to give required notice under the lease or the law.
- Wrong amount of rent claimed: the landlord is claiming the wrong rent amount or failed to credit payments.
- Wrongful eviction: before the court case, the landlord did something illegal to try to force the tenant out, such as changing locks, cutting off utilities, or refusing repairs to pressure the tenant to leave.
- The tenant offered the rent on time but the landlord wrongfully refused to accept it.
- The tenant did not violate the lease or any enforceable house rules.

Being unable to pay rent because of hardship is generally not a legal defense.

If a tenant believes the landlord has harmed them, the tenant may file one or more counterclaims against the landlord. If a tenant wants to assert a counterclaim, it must be done in writing and filed before the trial.

Prepare for Court

Understand the legal reason the landlord is trying to evict you by reading the complaint carefully. Understand whether the landlord is also asking for a money judgment. Figure out your defenses. Gather your evidence, including the lease, rent receipts, pictures, inspection reports, texts, emails, and other relevant materials.

Important preparation steps include:

- Fill out an Answer or Counterclaims form if needed and file it with the clerk of court.
- Bring witnesses who have relevant first-hand knowledge.
- Make a list of the important facts and defenses you want to explain.
- Print pictures, texts, or emails from your phone before going to court.
- Bring three sets of important papers: one for the magistrate, one for the landlord, and one for yourself.
- If the landlord has not made repairs, consider contacting local code enforcement to ask for an inspection.
- Arrange child care if possible and avoid bringing small children to court.
- Dress neatly and arrive early.

The Eviction Hearing

At the hearing, both the tenant and the landlord can testify. Both sides can ask questions and show the magistrate receipts, pictures, and other evidence.

The magistrate must decide the case based on what is presented in court. The magistrate cannot consider information or evidence that was not presented during the hearing.

Speak slowly and clearly. Be respectful. Direct statements to the magistrate, not to the landlord. Use notes to make sure you explain all important facts, defenses, and claims. Show all important evidence to the magistrate.

Witnesses and Their Testimony

Witnesses may be brought to court if they have direct knowledge of relevant facts. A witness must come to court in person. Written witness statements are generally not enough. Witnesses should testify about things they personally saw or heard. Hearsay is generally not allowed. Good witnesses are people with first-hand knowledge who do not appear biased.

If necessary, a witness can be subpoenaed to appear.

Physical Evidence

Receipts, leases, letters, texts, emails, inspection reports, and photographs may be shown to the magistrate if they help prove the case. Organize the evidence before court and bring printed copies. Explain what each item shows.

Pictures should be clear and focused on important conditions. If pictures are used, explain what each picture shows and when it was taken.

The Magistrate's Decision

After hearing from both sides, the magistrate may make a decision immediately or may take a short time to decide. The magistrate's civil court decision is called a judgment. The judgment may state whether the tenant is to be evicted and whether any money is owed.

Settlement

At any time in the case, the tenant and landlord may reach an agreement. This is called a settlement. If the case is settled, the agreement should be put in writing, dated, and signed by both sides. Each side should keep a copy. The magistrate or clerk should also be informed as needed so the case can be handled correctly.

Ten-Day Appeal Period

After the magistrate makes a decision, either party may appeal within 10 days by filing a Notice of Appeal at the courthouse.

The appeal period is 10 calendar days. If the last day falls on a day when the courts are closed, the final day becomes the next day when the courts are open.

There is usually a $150 filing fee to appeal. A tenant who receives AFDC, Food Stamps, or SSI, or who otherwise qualifies as indigent, may ask for the fee to be waived by filing a Petition to Proceed as an Indigent. To avoid being evicted during the appeal, a tenant may also need to file a Bond to Stay Execution and pay future rent to the court while the appeal is pending.

The magistrate's judgment is not final for 10 calendar days. During that time, the tenant generally cannot be physically removed from the home unless the legal process continues and no valid appeal or bond prevents enforcement.

Padlocking

If the tenant does not appeal in time, or if required bond payments are not made, the landlord may obtain a Writ of Execution or Writ of Possession directing the sheriff to remove the tenant and change the locks.

Before padlocking, the tenant should receive notice from the sheriff. If the tenant has not moved by the scheduled time, the sheriff may require the tenant to leave. The landlord does not have authority to physically remove the tenant without going through the court and sheriff process.

After removal, the tenant must arrange with the landlord to recover personal belongings within the time allowed by law. If the tenant does not do so, the landlord may later dispose of the belongings according to North Carolina law.

Important Words to Know

Answer: the legal document the tenant files to respond to the complaint.

Answer and Counterclaim: the tenant's written response to the landlord's complaint together with the tenant's own claims against the landlord.

Appeal: a request for a new hearing in district court after small claims court.

Complaint: the legal document that starts a civil court case.

Continuance: postponement of the court date.

Counterclaim: the tenant's claim against the landlord in the same lawsuit.

Defense: a legal reason why the plaintiff should not win the case.

Defendant: the person being sued.

Judgment: the court's decision.

Late fee: an extra charge for late rent, subject to legal limits.

Lease: a written or oral rental agreement.

Magistrate: the judge in small claims court.

Notice: a communication stating something important, such as a lease termination or repair request.

Plaintiff: the person who starts the case.

Rent abatement: reducing or refunding rent because the landlord failed to make required repairs.

Retaliatory eviction: an eviction filed because the tenant exercised legal rights, such as requesting repairs.

Summary ejectment: the legal term for eviction in North Carolina.

Waiver: giving up a legal right, such as when a landlord accepts rent after knowingly allowing a lease violation to continue.
